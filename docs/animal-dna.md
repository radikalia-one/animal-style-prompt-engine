# Animal DNA Specification

## 1. Purpose

Animal DNA is the structured description of the biological, anatomical, visual, and expressive characteristics that make an uploaded animal recognizable.

Its purpose is to preserve species identity while allowing the animal to be transformed into a different artistic style.

Style DNA determines:

"How should the animal look?"

Animal DNA determines:

"What must remain recognizable?"

Both systems must work together.

---

## 2. Core Principle

The transformation system must preserve the animal before applying stylization.

The following hierarchy should normally be respected:

1. Species identity.
2. Signature silhouette.
3. Essential anatomy.
4. Signature features.
5. Facial identity.
6. Expression.
7. Pose.
8. Natural markings.
9. Natural color relationships.
10. Secondary biological details.

Stylization must never unintentionally transform the animal into another species.

---

## 3. Species Identification

The system should identify the animal as precisely as reasonably possible.

Examples:

- Wolf.
- Bengal tiger.
- Barn owl.
- Red-eyed tree frog.
- Rhinoceros beetle.
- Emperor penguin.
- Green iguana.

When exact subspecies identification is uncertain, the system should avoid inventing unsupported information.

---

## 4. Silhouette

The silhouette is one of the strongest indicators of animal identity.

Analyze:

- Overall body outline.
- Head profile.
- Back curvature.
- Neck length.
- Torso shape.
- Limb placement.
- Tail profile.
- Wing profile.
- Ear profile.
- Horn configuration.
- Fin configuration.
- Characteristic negative spaces.

Identify which silhouette characteristics must survive stylization.

---

## 5. Body Proportions

Analyze relationships between major body parts.

Examples:

- Head-to-body ratio.
- Torso length.
- Body width.
- Neck-to-body ratio.
- Limb length.
- Leg thickness.
- Tail-to-body ratio.
- Wing-to-body ratio.
- Ear-to-head ratio.
- Horn-to-head ratio.

Record proportions comparatively rather than relying only on exact measurements.

Examples:

- Very large head relative to body.
- Long narrow limbs.
- Compact torso.
- Extremely short neck.
- Oversized ears.

---

## 6. Head Structure

Analyze:

- Skull shape.
- Forehead profile.
- Cheek volume.
- Jaw shape.
- Muzzle length.
- Snout width.
- Beak geometry.
- Nose position.
- Eye position.
- Ear placement.
- Horn placement.

The transformed design should preserve the primary structure of the head.

---

## 7. Eyes

Analyze:

- Size.
- Shape.
- Color.
- Spacing.
- Orientation.
- Position.
- Protrusion.
- Pupil shape.
- Iris visibility.
- Expression.

Some species depend heavily on eye characteristics for recognition.

Examples:

Red-eyed tree frog:

- Large protruding eyes.
- Bright red iris.
- Dark vertical pupil.

Owl:

- Large forward-facing eyes.

Goat:

- Horizontal pupils.

These characteristics should not be replaced by generic eye designs without considering species identity.

---

## 8. Nose, Muzzle, Snout, or Beak

Depending on species, analyze:

- Nose geometry.
- Muzzle length.
- Snout taper.
- Beak curvature.
- Nostril position.
- Mouth relationship.
- Jaw shape.

Stylization may simplify these forms but should preserve their species-specific structure.

---

## 9. Ears

When present, analyze:

- Size.
- Shape.
- Orientation.
- Placement.
- Thickness.
- Degree of mobility.
- Signature tips or folds.

Examples:

- Long rabbit ears.
- Rounded bear ears.
- Triangular fox ears.
- Large elephant ears.

Ears can strongly affect silhouette identity.

---

## 10. Horns, Antlers, Crests, and Tusks

Analyze when present:

- Quantity.
- Length.
- Direction.
- Curvature.
- Branching.
- Position.
- Symmetry.
- Thickness.

These features frequently act as primary species identifiers.

They must not be removed unless explicitly requested.

---

## 11. Limbs

Analyze:

- Number of limbs.
- Relative length.
- Thickness.
- Joint placement.
- Orientation.
- Locomotion type.

Possible locomotion categories include:

- Quadruped.
- Biped.
- Arboreal.
- Swimming.
- Flying.
- Crawling.
- Hopping.

Never add or remove limbs accidentally.

---

## 12. Feet, Paws, Hooves, and Claws

Analyze:

- Paw shape.
- Toe count when visually important.
- Hoof structure.
- Claw visibility.
- Webbing.
- Toe pads.
- Adhesive pads.
- Talons.

Examples:

Tree frog:
- Large adhesive toe pads.

Bird of prey:
- Strong curved talons.

Horse:
- Hooves rather than paws.

These details may be simplified but should not be replaced with anatomy belonging to another animal.

---

## 13. Wings

When present, analyze:

- Wingspan.
- Wing shape.
- Folded or extended state.
- Feather grouping.
- Membrane structure.
- Relationship to torso.

Differentiate between:

- Bird wings.
- Bat wings.
- Insect wings.

Do not interchange their anatomy.

---

## 14. Tail

Analyze:

- Length.
- Thickness.
- Curvature.
- Position.
- Fur distribution.
- Fin structure.
- Segmentation.
- Signature tip.

Examples:

- Fox: large bushy tail.
- Beaver: broad flattened tail.
- Scorpion: segmented tail.
- Seahorse: curled prehensile tail.

The tail may be a primary silhouette identifier.

---

## 15. Skin, Fur, Feathers, Scales, or Exoskeleton

Identify the animal's natural surface system.

Possible categories:

- Fur.
- Hair.
- Feathers.
- Scales.
- Smooth skin.
- Rough skin.
- Exoskeleton.
- Shell.
- Plates.
- Spines.

This information does not necessarily need to remain literal after stylization.

Instead it may influence how Style DNA translates the surface.

Example:

Fur
→ sculpted volume or graphic color regions.

Feathers
→ layered simplified panels.

Scales
→ shallow geometric segmentation.

Exoskeleton
→ large molded shell sections.

---

## 16. Natural Color Palette

Identify:

- Dominant colors.
- Secondary colors.
- Accent colors.
- Eye colors.
- Limb colors.
- Belly colors.
- Signature markings.

Also identify relationships between colors.

Example:

Red-eyed tree frog:

Dominant:
- Bright green.

Secondary:
- Blue and yellow side pattern.

Accents:
- Red eyes.
- Orange feet.

Neutral:
- Pale underside.

The transformed style may simplify the palette while preserving important identity colors.

---

## 17. Markings and Patterns

Analyze:

- Stripes.
- Spots.
- Bands.
- Patches.
- Gradients.
- Eye markings.
- Facial markings.
- Wing markings.
- Tail markings.

Classify markings by importance.

### Identity-critical markings

Strongly associated with the animal.

### Secondary markings

Useful but not essential.

### Optional micro-patterns

May be removed during simplification.

---

## 18. Signature Features

Identify the smallest set of characteristics necessary for immediate recognition.

Examples:

Elephant:

- Trunk.
- Large ears.
- Massive body.

Giraffe:

- Long neck.
- Ossicones.
- Patch pattern.

Rhino beetle:

- Large characteristic horn.
- Compact beetle shell.

Toucan:

- Oversized colorful beak.

Red-eyed tree frog:

- Red protruding eyes.
- Bright green body.
- Orange toe pads.

These become high-priority preservation rules.

---

## 19. Pose

Analyze:

- Standing.
- Sitting.
- Walking.
- Running.
- Flying.
- Swimming.
- Crouching.
- Climbing.
- Resting.
- Hunting.

Also analyze:

- Body orientation.
- Head direction.
- Limb placement.
- Weight distribution.
- Gesture.

Unless otherwise requested, the generated prompt should try to preserve the recognizable pose of the uploaded image.

---

## 20. Expression

Analyze the apparent expression without unnecessarily humanizing the animal.

Possible descriptions:

- Calm.
- Alert.
- Curious.
- Defensive.
- Playful.
- Intense.
- Relaxed.
- Focused.

The system should preserve visually observable expression rather than inventing human emotions unsupported by the image.

---

## 21. Personality Cues

Visual characteristics may suggest design personality.

Examples:

- Compact + large eyes → approachable.
- Tall posture + narrow eyes → alert.
- Massive silhouette → powerful.
- Rounded body → gentle.
- Long limbs → agile.

These observations can influence stylization but must remain secondary to anatomy.

---

## 22. Identity Priority Classification

Animal characteristics should be classified into three levels.

### Tier A — Immutable

Changing these may destroy species identity.

Examples:

- Trunk on elephant.
- Long neck on giraffe.
- Main horn on rhinoceros.
- Characteristic beak on toucan.

### Tier B — Important

Should normally be preserved but may be simplified.

Examples:

- Natural markings.
- Limb thickness.
- Ear details.
- Secondary colors.

### Tier C — Flexible

Can change significantly according to the selected style.

Examples:

- Surface texture.
- Fine fur.
- Tiny scales.
- Micro-details.
- Minor wrinkles.

---

## 23. Style Translation Rules

Animal DNA should not prescribe the final artistic material.

It provides biological information that Style DNA translates.

Examples:

Animal DNA:

"Thick striped fur"

Style DNA:

"Smooth injection-molded polymer"

Final translation:

"Smooth sculpted polymer body with large graphic stripe panels."

---

Animal DNA:

"Layered owl feathers"

Style DNA:

"Minimal sofubi sculpture"

Final translation:

"Large smooth wing volumes with shallow feather-inspired sculptural divisions."

---

Animal DNA:

"Tree frog adhesive toe pads"

Style DNA:

"Mechanical designer toy"

Final translation:

"Rounded elastomer toe modules preserving the frog's characteristic oversized pads."

---

## 24. Mutation Prevention

The system must actively avoid:

- Wrong species anatomy.
- Extra limbs.
- Missing limbs.
- Incorrect wing type.
- Incorrect paw type.
- Incorrect tail.
- Incorrect ear placement.
- Wrong beak.
- Wrong horn configuration.
- Generic humanoid anatomy.
- Unnecessary human facial structure.

Stylization is allowed.

Unintentional biological mutation is not.
