# Style DNA Specification

## 1. Purpose

Style DNA is the structured description of the reusable visual rules found in one or more style reference images.

It does not describe the specific subject shown in the reference.

It describes the artistic system behind the image.

The purpose of Style DNA is to allow the same visual language to be adapted to many different animals while producing consistent and original results.

---

## 2. Core Principle

The system must separate:

- Subject identity.
- Artistic style.

The reference image may show a specific animal, character, object, or toy.

The Style DNA must not copy that subject.

It should extract only reusable visual properties such as:

- Shape language.
- Proportions.
- Materials.
- Color organization.
- Surface treatment.
- Simplification.
- Lighting.
- Rendering.
- Composition.
- Construction logic.

---

## 3. Shape Language

Shape language describes the types of shapes used throughout the design.

The system should identify:

- Rounded or angular forms.
- Soft or hard transitions.
- Large or small corner radii.
- Symmetrical or asymmetrical construction.
- Inflated, compact, elongated, flat, or tapered volumes.
- Organic or geometric forms.
- Continuous or segmented surfaces.
- Repeated shapes and visual motifs.

Questions to answer:

- Are the primary forms spherical, cylindrical, box-like, or irregular?
- Are the edges sharp or rounded?
- Do the shapes feel soft, rigid, mechanical, sculptural, or natural?
- Is the silhouette simple or complex?
- Are there repeated circular, oval, triangular, or cone-shaped elements?

---

## 4. Volume Hierarchy

Volume hierarchy describes how the design distributes visual mass.

The system should separate:

### Primary forms

The largest shapes that define the silhouette.

Examples:

- Main body.
- Head.
- Torso.
- Large shell.
- Large wings.
- Main tail mass.

### Secondary forms

Medium-sized forms that support the anatomy.

Examples:

- Limbs.
- Ears.
- Muzzle.
- Beak.
- Horns.
- Fins.
- Eye housings.

### Tertiary forms

Small details that provide refinement.

Examples:

- Small seams.
- Vents.
- Buttons.
- Surface markings.
- Tiny mechanical modules.
- Minor facial details.

The primary forms must remain visually dominant.

Tertiary details must never overpower the silhouette.

---

## 5. Proportion System

The proportion system describes how body parts relate to each other.

The analysis should identify:

- Head-to-body ratio.
- Eye-to-head ratio.
- Limb length.
- Body width.
- Neck length.
- Tail length.
- Ear, horn, fin, or wing scale.
- Degree of exaggeration.
- Degree of anatomical simplification.

The system should determine whether the style uses:

- Realistic proportions.
- Childlike proportions.
- Chibi proportions.
- Toy-like proportions.
- Heroic proportions.
- Compact proportions.
- Elongated proportions.

The animal must remain recognizable even when proportions are stylized.

---

## 6. Silhouette Rules

The silhouette must communicate the animal species before internal details are considered.

The system should identify:

- The most recognizable outer contours.
- Characteristic ears, horns, tails, fins, wings, or body profiles.
- Important negative spaces.
- Distinctive posture.
- Balance between simplification and recognition.

A successful transformation must preserve the most important silhouette features of the uploaded animal.

---

## 7. Simplification Rules

Simplification determines how biological complexity becomes a cleaner visual design.

The system should identify:

- Which natural details are removed.
- Which details are combined.
- Which details are enlarged.
- Which details become color blocks.
- Which textures become panels or shallow relief.
- Which anatomical forms are represented by a single sculptural volume.

Possible translations include:

- Fur into smooth surfaces or large color regions.
- Feathers into layered panels.
- Scales into shallow segmentation.
- Skin folds into clean transitions.
- Muscle groups into rounded masses.
- Natural markings into painted graphic shapes.

Simplification must not erase species identity.

---

## 8. Color System

The color system describes how color is selected and organized.

The analysis should identify:

- Dominant color.
- Secondary color.
- Accent color.
- Neutral structural color.
- Number of major colors.
- Saturation level.
- Contrast level.
- Warm or cool balance.
- Gradient usage.
- Hard or soft paint boundaries.
- Natural or invented palette.
- Placement of accent colors.

The system should determine whether color is used to:

- Define anatomy.
- Separate materials.
- Frame the face.
- Highlight important features.
- Create visual rhythm.
- Guide the viewer's attention.

Color should not be distributed randomly.

---

## 9. Material System

The material system describes what the object appears to be made from.

Possible materials include:

- Soft vinyl.
- Injection-molded plastic.
- Painted resin.
- Rubber.
- Metal.
- Glass.
- Ceramic.
- Clay.
- Wood.
- Fabric.
- Biological surfaces.

The analysis should identify:

- Gloss level.
- Roughness.
- Reflectivity.
- Transparency.
- Surface softness.
- Surface hardness.
- Material separation.
- Whether the object appears new, worn, handmade, or manufactured.

Materials should be described with precise visual language.

Avoid vague words such as:

- Nice.
- Cool.
- Beautiful.
- High quality.

Prefer descriptions such as:

- Satin injection-molded polymer.
- Matte soft-touch elastomer.
- Glossy smoked-black optical lens.
- Semi-gloss painted resin.
- Dense molded rubber.

---

## 10. Surface Treatment

Surface treatment describes how details appear on the outer shell.

The system should identify:

- Smooth or textured surfaces.
- Panel lines.
- Recessed seams.
- Shallow relief.
- Painted markings.
- Molded details.
- Scratches or wear.
- Clean or distressed finish.
- Biological or synthetic texture.

The amount of surface detail must match the reference style.

Minimal styles should not receive excessive detail.

Detailed styles should not become visually empty.

---

## 11. Facial Construction

The face is one of the most important areas for preserving identity.

The Style DNA should describe:

- Eye size.
- Eye shape.
- Eye spacing.
- Eye material.
- Eyelid treatment.
- Brow construction.
- Nose, muzzle, or beak construction.
- Mouth shape.
- Expression intensity.
- Degree of humanization.
- Degree of abstraction.

The system must preserve the expression and recognizable facial structure of the uploaded animal.

It must not force every species into the same generic face.

---

## 12. Construction Logic

Construction logic explains how the design appears to be assembled or manufactured.

Possible systems include:

- Single-piece sculpture.
- Soft-vinyl toy.
- Injection-molded shell.
- Modular mechanical assembly.
- Layered armor.
- Hand-sculpted clay.
- Folded paper.
- Carved wood.
- Inflatable form.

The analysis should identify:

- Visible seams.
- Hidden joints.
- Panel divisions.
- Part intersections.
- Manufacturing symmetry.
- Articulation.
- Mold-friendly geometry.
- Decorative versus functional details.

Every detail should support the selected construction logic.

---

## 13. Detail Density

Detail density describes how much visual information appears in different areas.

The system should determine:

- Overall level of detail.
- Areas of high detail.
- Areas of visual rest.
- Repetition of small details.
- Balance between simple and complex areas.

Good designs usually contain:

- Large simple areas.
- A few controlled focal points.
- Limited tertiary detail.
- Clear visual hierarchy.

Avoid uniform detail across the entire character.

---

## 14. Visual Rhythm

Visual rhythm describes how forms, colors, and details repeat across the design.

Examples include:

- Repeating circular modules.
- Alternating color panels.
- Repeated soft cones.
- Consistent seam spacing.
- Repeated curves.
- Gradual changes in size.

The rhythm should feel intentional rather than random.

---

## 15. Lighting System

The lighting system describes how the subject is illuminated.

The analysis should identify:

- Key-light direction.
- Light softness.
- Fill-light strength.
- Rim-light presence.
- Shadow softness.
- Highlight width.
- Ambient occlusion.
- Contact shadows.
- Background illumination.
- Studio or environmental lighting.

Lighting must support the materials and shape readability.

---

## 16. Rendering System

The rendering system describes the final visual presentation.

Possible rendering styles include:

- Premium 3D CGI.
- Product visualization.
- Photorealistic rendering.
- Stylized animation rendering.
- Clay render.
- Illustration.
- Flat graphic design.
- Hand-painted concept art.

The analysis should identify:

- Realism level.
- Stylization level.
- Lens behavior.
- Depth of field.
- Reflection quality.
- Shadow quality.
- Edge sharpness.
- Image cleanliness.
- Background complexity.

---

## 17. Composition System

Composition describes how the character is framed.

The system should identify:

- Camera angle.
- Subject orientation.
- Full body or close-up.
- Centered or offset framing.
- Eye-level, low-angle, or high-angle view.
- Lens feel.
- Background type.
- Amount of empty space.
- Product-shot or narrative presentation.

The composition should clearly display the transformed animal.

---

## 18. Style Priority Order

When generating a prompt, the system should follow this order:

1. Preserve species identity.
2. Preserve signature silhouette.
3. Preserve recognizable anatomy.
4. Preserve expression and pose.
5. Apply shape language.
6. Apply proportion system.
7. Apply material system.
8. Apply color system.
9. Apply surface treatment.
10. Apply construction details.
11. Apply lighting.
12. Apply rendering.
13. Apply composition.

The system must never sacrifice species identity merely to increase stylization.

---

## 19. Negative Style Rules

Style DNA should also record what must not appear.

Examples:

- Photorealistic fur.
- Biological pores.
- Excessive mechanical details.
- Random armor.
- Exposed wires.
- Uncontrolled textures.
- Sharp spikes.
- Dirty surfaces.
- Wrong materials.
- Excessive color variation.
- Humanoid anatomy.
- Extra limbs.
- Missing limbs.
- Generic robot design.
- Direct copying of the reference subject.

Negative rules help prevent style drift.
